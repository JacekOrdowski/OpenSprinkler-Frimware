# Ethical engagement and screen time for a children's math game (Poland/EU, ages ~7–15), research notes as of October 2026

Scope note: these notes cover (a) manipulative/dark design patterns in children's apps, (b) loot boxes, variable rewards and streaks, (c) ethical ways to keep a game engaging, including stopping points, (d) screen-time guidance and the quality-vs-quantity debate, (e) EU/Polish/UK/Apple rules, and (f) a concrete do/don't list. Search tools returned many secondary summaries. Where I could not open the primary document, I say so. Dates of every guideline are given because several changed in 2025–2026.

## 1. Which design patterns in children's apps count as manipulative ("dark patterns")?

### Takeaway
Peer-reviewed content analyses (Radesky et al. 2022, Meyer et al. 2019) show that manipulative design is the norm in popular children's apps. Only about 1 in 5 apps used by preschoolers had none. The recurring patterns are: lures (rewards and sparkles at the moment the child decides whether to stop or buy), navigation constraints (hidden exits, forced auto-advance), parasocial pressure (characters who guilt or shame the child), fabricated time pressure, and ads timed to interrupt or to fire when the child tries to leave. Regulators (EU Commission 2025, the UK ICO) and child-rights groups (5Rights) now name the same features (streaks, autoplay, push notifications, reward loops) as things to switch off by default for minors.

### Cited Findings
- **Radesky et al. 2022, JAMA Network Open (cross-sectional).** 160 children aged 3–5 (mean 4.0) with their own devices; 133 unique apps; each child's 3 longest-used apps were coded by two coders, who played each app for 10–15 minutes against 11 manipulative-design codes — [PMC9206186](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9206186/)
- Radesky taxonomy and definitions — [PMC9206186](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9206186/):
  - *Parasocial relationship pressure:* characters shame, taunt or pressure children to keep playing or to buy. Example: Talking Tom asks whether the player wants to "give up".
  - *Time pressure:* fabricated urgency (countdown clocks, "limited time" messages), usually placed at decision points.
  - *Navigation constraints:* blocked or hidden exits, forced auto-advance, pop-ups, opt-out buttons made hard to see.
  - *Lures:* rewards, trophies, sparkles and pulsing buttons that grab attention just as the child decides what to do next.
  - *Advertising pressure:* "roadblock" ads that are hard to close, ads triggered when the child tries to leave or declines a purchase, and ads that pay in-game rewards for viewing.
- Radesky prevalence figures — [PMC9206186](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9206186/):
  - Only 19.6% of apps had no manipulative feature, and 98.8% of children had at least one in their top apps.
  - Gameplay-prolonging features were in 64.7% of apps and reached 95.0% of children. Within that group: lures 45.1% of apps, navigation constraints 45.9%, parasocial pressure 24.8%, time pressure 17.3%.
  - Purchase-encouraging features were in 55.6% of apps and reached 80.0% of children.
  - Advertising of any type was in 31.6% of apps; roadblock ads in 21.8%.
- Free apps had more manipulative features than paid ones (median 3 vs 1). General-audience apps scored highest. PBS KIDS apps had **no manipulative features other than autoplay**, and educational and early-childhood apps scored significantly lower — [PMC9206186](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9206186/)
- Equity finding (exploratory): children of parents without a college degree had higher total scores (median 3.7 vs 3.0) and higher purchase-pressure scores (1.0 vs 0.6) — [PMC9206186](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9206186/)
- Radesky's recommendations: ethical standards for purchase-related design, more regulation of mobile ad networks, and consideration of children's needs before launch, because many teams lack child-development expertise. The paper points to the UK Age Appropriate Design Code and the EU DSA — [PMC9206186](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9206186/)
- Limitations: convenience sample (75% non-Hispanic White, 60% college-educated parents); only the longest-used apps were coded, which may overstate prevalence. The lead author disclosed a past paid advisory role with Noggin, an app that was in the sample — [PMC9206186](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9206186/); [Deceptive.design summary](https://deceptive.design/articles/prevalence-and-characteristics-of-manipulative-design-in-mobile-applications-used-by-children)
- **Meyer et al., "Advertising in Young Children's Apps: A Content Analysis"** (J Dev Behav Pediatr, Jan 2019; reported Oct 2018):
  - 135 popular free and paid apps from the Google Play "5 & Under" category; 129 (95%) contained at least one type of advertising, including 88% of paid apps.
  - Ad types: pop-up videos interrupting play, purchase prompts voiced by commercial characters, banners, and hidden ads disguised as game items.
  - Some apps requested phone, microphone, camera or location permissions.
  - The authors note that children aged 8 and under struggle to tell content from advertising.
  - Sources: [Michigan Medicine](https://www.michiganmedicine.org/health-lab/advertising-kids-apps-more-prevalent-parents-may-realize); [paper PDF](https://blogs.ubc.ca/etec523/files/2021/01/00004703-201901000-00004.pdf)
- **5Rights Foundation, "Disrupted Childhood: The Cost of Persuasive Design"** (2018; updated v2 2023):
  - Defines persuasive design as combining behavioural science, psychology and technology to maximise engagement, keeping children online and nudging them to give up more data. Techniques named: reward loops, notifications and personalised recommendations.
  - The 2023 edition considers effects on mental health, relationships, sleep, education and autonomy. It says the 2018 features are still common, and that progress since then came mainly from regulation (the AADC), not industry self-reform.
  - It argues responsibility should not sit mainly with children and parents.
  - Sources: [5Rights 2023 page](https://5rightsfoundation.com/in-action/disrupted-childhood-the-cost-of-persuasive-design-2023.html); [PDF v2](https://5rightsfoundation.com/uploads/Disrupted-Childhood-2023-v2.pdf); [EU Better Internet for Kids summary](https://better-internet-for-kids.europa.eu/en/knowledge-hub/stakeholder-policy-contributions/disrupted-childhood-cost-persuasive-design)
- **EU Commission guidelines under DSA Art. 28 (14 July 2025)**, per summaries:
  - Platforms should disable by default for minors the features that contribute to excessive use. Examples named in summaries: messaging streaks, ephemeral content, read receipts, autoplay and push notifications.
  - Persuasive features aimed predominantly at engagement should be removed.
  - The guidelines also cover loot boxes and virtual currencies that can lead to unwanted spending or addictive behaviour.
  - Sources: [EAO Merlin](https://merlin.obs.coe.int/article/10383); [Scalevise summary](https://scalevise.com/resources/eu-dsa-guidelines-addictive-design-minors/). Freshfields adds: push notifications off by default and always off during "core sleep hours"; avoid manipulative design in commercial practices; persuasive design choices must be assessed through a safety-by-design lens and justified — [Freshfields](https://www.freshfields.com/en/our-thinking/blogs/technology-quotient/dsa-decoded-6-the-european-commission-finalises-guidelines-on-the-protection-of-102kv4s)
- **European Parliament resolution on protection of minors online** (26 Nov 2025, non-binding):
  - Calls for a ban on the most harmful addictive practices, and for other addictive features to be disabled by default for minors. Named features: infinite scrolling, autoplay, pull-to-refresh, reward loops, harmful gamification.
  - Wants manipulative features addressed in the future Digital Fairness Act.
  - Sources: [Digital Policy Alert](https://digitalpolicyalert.org/event/35723-european-parliament-adopted-resolution-on-protection-of-minors-online-20252060ini); [EU Perspectives](https://euperspectives.eu/2025/11/eu-parliament-pushes-for-social-media-majority-age-of-16/)
- **UK ICO Age Appropriate Design Code, Standard 13 "Nudge techniques":**
  - Defines nudges as "design features which lead or encourage users to follow the designer's preferred paths". Example: a big "yes" button next to a small "no".
  - Prohibits nudging children toward giving unnecessary data or weakening privacy settings, and warns against exploiting unconscious psychological processes such as colour and imagery associations.
  - The Code applies to apps and games that process personal data and are likely to be accessed by UK children.
  - Sources: [ICO standard 13](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/guide-to-data-protection/ico-codes-of-practice/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/13-nudge-techniques) (the page now returns 404; content as reported in search snippets); [ICO code PDF v2.1](https://ico.org.uk/media/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services-2-1.pdf)
- **Apple App Review Guideline 5.6:** apps "should never prey on users or attempt to rip off customers, trick them into making unwanted purchases, force them to share unnecessary data… or engage in any other manipulative practices". Guideline 3.2.2(x): apps must not force users to rate or review the app in order to unlock functionality — [Apple App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- Parents' accounts link autoplay and "suggested videos" to many screen-time battles with young children — [University of Washington, Hiniker et al. 2016](https://www.washington.edu/news/2016/05/05/two-minute-warnings-make-kids-screen-time-tantrums-worse/)

### Inferences
- A practical working definition for this project: a design pattern is manipulative when it serves the product's metrics (time, return visits, spending, data) at the moment a child is deciding to stop, and works through pressure, guilt, urgency, hidden exits or unpredictability rather than through the child's own interest in the activity.
- The two things that most reliably separate low-manipulation apps (PBS KIDS, educational apps) from high-manipulation ones are the absence of a monetization model (ads or in-app purchases) and the absence of any business need to maximise session time. A free, non-commercial game with no ads and no purchases removes most of the incentive for dark patterns by construction.
- Feedback such as stars and sparkles is not manipulative in itself. Radesky codes it as a "lure" when it appears at the decision to stop or buy, so timing and context matter.

### Gaps
- I could not open the full text of the EU Commission guidelines (Communication C(2025) 4764, or similar reference). The streak, autoplay and loot-box examples come from secondary summaries (EAO Merlin, Scalevise, Freshfields, 5Rights analysis). The guidelines said they would be reviewed "at the latest after 12 months", so a revision may have happened around July 2026. I found none.
- No equivalent content analysis exists for apps used by school-age children (7–15). The Radesky and Meyer studies cover ages 0–5.
- I did not find an FTC staff report specific to dark patterns and children in this pass. (FTC "Bringing Dark Patterns to Light", Sept 2022, exists but was not verified here.)

## 2. Evidence on loot boxes, variable-ratio rewards and streaks

### Takeaway
Consistent correlational evidence links loot-box spending with problem-gambling scores, and the link is stronger in adolescents than in adults. Regulation is fragmented:
- Belgium treats paid loot boxes as illegal gambling, but enforcement is weak.
- The Dutch top court overturned the main Dutch enforcement case in 2022.
- The European Parliament asked in November 2025 for an EU ban on randomised paid features for minors.
- Apple requires odds disclosure.

Streaks have almost no rigorous child-specific evidence. Their effect on learning is "open to debate", and EU bodies already list them among features to disable by default for minors.

### Cited Findings
- Zendle & Cairns 2018 (PLoS ONE): survey of 7,422 gamers found a link between loot-box spending and problem-gambling severity, η² = 0.054. The authors called this stronger than links with common risk factors such as drug abuse — [Zendle & Cairns written evidence, UK Parliament](https://committees.parliament.uk/writtenevidence/98535/html/); [White Rose eprint](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/139116)
- Zendle, Meyer & Over 2019 (Royal Society Open Science): survey of 16–18-year-olds (n = 1,155) found a loot-box–problem-gambling link of η² = 0.120, about twice the adult effect. Correlational, not causal — [PMC6599795](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6599795); [White Rose](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/document/1493576)
- Zendle & Cairns told the UK Parliament that loot boxes either cause problem gambling or exploit existing problem gamblers. This is their interpretation, not a tested causal finding — [UK Parliament written evidence](https://committees.parliament.uk/writtenevidence/98535/html/)
- **Close & Lloyd 2021, "Lifting the Lid on Loot-Boxes"** (GambleAware-funded; Universities of Plymouth and Wolverhampton):
  - Loot boxes are "structurally and psychologically akin to gambling", and 12 of 13 surveys found a positive correlation with problem gambling.
  - About 5% of purchasers generate about half of loot-box revenue, and about a third of those are problem gamblers. Younger men and people with lower education buy more.
  - Recommendations: enforceable age ratings, odds disclosure, spending limits, and prices shown in real currency.
  - Sources: [GambleAware](https://gambleaware.org/our-research/publication-library/articles/lifting-the-lid-on-loot-boxes); [PC Games Insider](https://www.pcgamesinsider.biz/news/72079/uk-charity-gambleaware-says-loot-boxes-structurally-and-psychologically-akin-to-gambling/)
- **Belgium (2018):** the Gaming Commission treated paid loot boxes as gambling, with criminal prosecution possible; in practice no licence is obtainable.
  - Xiao's study found the ban poorly enforced: 82 of the 100 highest-grossing Belgian iPhone games still had loot boxes. Sources disagree on whether the sample month was May 2021 or May 2022.
  - Xiao recommends against blanket bans.
  - Sources: [QMUL](https://qmul.ac.uk/law/news/2022/items/study-claims-country-wide-loot-box-ban-isnt-practically-achievable.html); [ITU Copenhagen](https://pure.itu.dk/en/publications/breaking-ban-belgiums-ineffective-gambling-law-regulation-of-vide/); [PCI Registered Reports](https://rr.peercommunityin.org/articles/rec?id=264)
- **Netherlands:** in March 2022 the Raad van State overturned the Kansspelautoriteit's penalty payment (up to €10m) against EA over FIFA Ultimate Team packs. Its reasoning: pack opening is not a self-contained game of chance but part of a skill game — [Raad van State](https://www.raadvanstate.nl/actueel/nieuws/@130150/202005769-1-a3/); [SBC News](https://sbcnews.co.uk/europe/uk/2022/03/10/ea-wins-long-standing-loot-box-drama-against-dutch-ksa/); [Taxlive](https://www.taxlive.nl/nl/documenten/nieuws/raad-van-state-lootboxes-in-fifa-game-zijn-toch-geen-kansspel/)
- **European Parliament, 26 Nov 2025:** urges the Commission to prohibit loot boxes and other randomised content (wheels of fortune, prize wheels) in games accessible to minors. The vote was 483 for and 86 abstentions; sources disagree on the number against (92 or 82) — [Digital Policy Alert](https://digitalpolicyalert.org/event/35723-european-parliament-adopted-resolution-on-protection-of-minors-online-20252060ini); [Politis](https://en.politis.com.cy/social-lens/973176/eu-parliament-declares-the-internet-strictly-unsafe-for-children)
- **EU Commission DSA Art. 28 guidelines (July 2025):** children's lack of commercial literacy should not be exploited "to avoid leading to unwanted spending or addictive behaviours, including certain virtual currencies or loot-boxes" — [Taylor Wessing](https://www.taylorwessing.com/en/insights-and-events/insights/2025/10/beyond-the-tech-giants); [EAO Merlin](https://merlin.obs.coe.int/article/10383)
- **Apple Guideline 3.1.1:** apps offering loot boxes or other randomised virtual items for purchase must disclose the odds of each item type before purchase — [Apple](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- **Streaks:**
  - A 2026 Northwestern IPR working paper on showing students their streaks says streaks are common, but "its impact remains open to debate". It discusses loss aversion as a possible amplifier and notes streaks can become goals in themselves — [Northwestern IPR WP-26-05](https://www.ipr.northwestern.edu/documents/working-papers/2026/wp-26-05.pdf)
  - Industry claims of 20–40% engagement uplift from streaks, and a "60%" Duolingo widget figure, come from vendor blogs without primary sources — [MWM glossary](https://mwm.ai/glossary/daily-streak) (unreliable; cited only to flag the claim)
  - EU guidelines summaries list "messaging streaks" among features to switch off by default for minors — [EAO Merlin](https://merlin.obs.coe.int/article/10383)

### Inferences
- For a free game with no purchases, the gambling-law question is moot. The *mechanic* of unpredictable rewards ("chests", "spin the wheel", random rare stickers) still borrows the variable-ratio structure that the gambling literature worries about. The European Parliament now wants randomised features banned for minors, even unpaid "wheels of fortune". The safest choice is predictable, earned rewards: you know what you get for finishing a level.
- Streaks work by loss aversion (fear of breaking the chain). For a children's learning game they risk turning practice into an obligation and creating guilt on missed days, which is the "pressure to return" the EU wants off by default. If any continuity indicator is used, it should not punish gaps. Examples: a cumulative count ("you have practised on 23 days") rather than a consecutive streak, or a streak that "freezes" automatically with no penalty or nagging.

### Gaps
- UK DCMS's July 2022 government response on loot boxes was not found in this search. From memory it declined gambling-law regulation and backed industry-led measures (Ukie principles, 2023). Not verified, so do not cite.
- No peer-reviewed study was found on streaks and compulsive use in children specifically.
- No study was found on *non-purchasable* random rewards (free daily chests) and children's wellbeing. The concern is extrapolated from gambling and behavioural psychology.

## 3. Healthy stopping points, session limits and ethical engagement in practice

### Takeaway
Children stop more easily at natural stopping points (the end of a level or story) and when stopping feels like their own choice. Autoplay and "next up" lures make stopping harder. Surprisingly, a parent's two-minute warning made preschoolers *more* upset.

Self-determination theory explains lasting engagement without tricks: games are enjoyable when they satisfy competence, autonomy and relatedness. Well-regarded educational apps follow this model. Khan Academy Kids is free with no ads or subscriptions; PBS KIDS apps had no manipulative features other than autoplay. Evidence that playful math games produce actual learning is mixed (DragonBox).

### Cited Findings
- **Hiniker et al. 2016 (CHI, "Screen Time Tantrums"):** interviews with 27 parents plus a diary study with 28 families of children aged 1–5.
  - What helped: routines, stopping at a natural stopping point, or the device "running out" (battery, Wi-Fi). Blaming the technology also reduced upset.
  - What backfired: a parent's two-minute warning made children *more* upset; autoplay and suggested videos drove many battles.
  - Source: [University of Washington News](https://www.washington.edu/news/2016/05/05/two-minute-warnings-make-kids-screen-time-tantrums-worse/)
- Follow-up design work: in a child video app tested with families of 3–5-year-olds, children first chose how long to watch and what to do afterwards. When they remembered their own plan, stopping felt like their choice. These details come from secondary blog summaries; attribution between UW and a Tilburg pilot is unclear — [UW CHiLL lab](https://depts.washington.edu/chilllab/?p=178); [MeeVee blog](https://meevee.com/blog/can-we-design-smoother-transitions-away-from-screens) (secondary)
- **Ryan, Rigby & Przybylski 2006:** the needs for autonomy, competence and relatedness independently predicted enjoyment and future play. This work led to the PENS measure — [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2006/12/061226134706.htm); [selfdeterminationtheory.org PENS](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/player-experience-of-needs-satisfaction-pens/)
- **Przybylski, Rigby & Ryan 2010, "A Motivational Model of Video Game Engagement"** (Review of General Psychology): both the appeal and the wellbeing effects of games depend on satisfying competence, autonomy and relatedness. The model also covers antecedents of disordered engagement — [Ewha repository](https://pure.ewha.ac.kr/en/publications/a-motivational-model-of-video-game-engagement/); [SAGE/APA doi 10.1037/a0019440](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1037/a0019440)
- **Khan Academy Kids:** officially free for ages 2–8 with no ads or subscriptions, funded as a nonprofit through donations and grants. Its design uses guide characters (Kodi Bear), short lessons and visual rewards, and was developed with Stanford learning experts — [Khan Academy Kids official](https://learn.khanacademy.org/khan-academy-kids/); [Hoot Reading review](https://learn.hootreading.com/blog/app-spotlight-khan-academy-kids) (secondary)
- **DragonBox (algebra):**
  - Engagement is well documented; learning evidence is mixed.
  - A Carnegie Mellon comparison with an intelligent tutor called it "a lot more smoke than fire" for learning, saying it gave too much scaffolding — [CMU](https://www.cmu.edu/homepage/computing/2014/spring/fun-and-games.shtml)
  - A design-based study found playful moments but limited algebraic knowledge gain — [Montclair](https://researchwith.montclair.edu/en/publications/a-design-based-approach-to-playful-algebra-learning-with-dragonbo/)
  - A Norwegian study of about 1,500 children reported better outcomes and attitudes. It was publicised by DragonBox's owner Kahoot, so there is a conflict of interest — [Kahoot](https://kahoot.com/kahoot-news/research-in-norway-shows-dragonbox-delivered-greater-learning-benefits-and-motivation/)
- PBS KIDS apps had no manipulative features other than autoplay in Radesky 2022, the cleanest category coded — [PMC9206186](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9206186/)
- The EU guidelines (2025) recommend that platforms prompt minors to explore new content after extended viewing, and that push notifications be off during sleep hours — [Freshfields](https://www.freshfields.com/en/our-thinking/blogs/technology-quotient/dsa-decoded-6-the-european-commission-finalises-guidelines-on-the-protection-of-102kv4s)
- The Canadian Paediatric Society (2019, reaffirmed 2025) favours activities that are "educational, active and social" over passive, solitary ones, and screens off at least one hour before bed — [CPS position statement](https://www.cps.ca/en/documents/position/digital-media); [PMC6736327](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6736327/)

### Inferences
- For a level-based math game, the natural stopping point is built in: the end of a level or world. The design question is what the game does at that moment. Ethical: a calm summary screen ("Well done! You finished level X"), an equally prominent "Finish for today" button, and no auto-start of the next level. Manipulative: a countdown to the next level, a reward that only unlocks if you continue, or a character saying "Don't go!".
- Session design: short, self-contained rounds (a few minutes each) map onto the natural-stopping-point evidence. A soft, child-friendly suggestion after a long session ("You've played for 30 minutes, time for a break?") follows the EU guidelines' "prompt after extended use" idea. Per Hiniker, framing it as the game's rule ("the dragon is tired now") may cause less upset than a countdown warning. That is an extrapolation from preschool data, not tested on ages 7–15.
- Ethical "hooks" that rely on SDT rather than compulsion:
  - competence: difficulty that adapts, visible mastery, clear feedback;
  - autonomy: the child chooses topic or level, with no forced path;
  - relatedness: playing with a parent or sibling, sharing progress with family offline.
- The DragonBox evidence is a caution: fun and engagement do not guarantee learning. Practice should map onto real curriculum skills (here, the Polish curriculum), and fun should come from the math itself, not from extrinsic slot-machine wrappers.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study was found on optimal session length for school-age educational games specifically.
- The Hiniker findings are for ages 1–5. I found no equivalent study on transitions for ages 7–15.
- I could not find published "ethical design principles" documents from Khan Academy Kids or DragonBox. The descriptions above come from official marketing and reviews.

## 4. Screen-time guidance (WHO, AAP, CPS, Poland) and the quality-vs-quantity debate

### Takeaway
No major body sets a hard numeric daily limit for school-age children any more:
- WHO (2020) says "limit recreational screen time" with no number for ages 5–17.
- The AAP's February 2026 policy explicitly dropped a set limit in favour of quality, family media plans and placing responsibility on product design.
- The Canadian Paediatric Society (2019, reaffirmed July 2025) uses four principles: manage, meaningful use, model, monitor.

I found no official numeric guideline from Poland's Ministry of Health or NASK. Poland's main 2026 action is a statutory ban on phone use in primary schools from 1 Sep 2026. The research debate (Haidt vs Odgers/Orben/Przybylski) is unresolved. Association sizes for screen time in general are small, and context and content matter more than minutes.

### Cited Findings
- **WHO 2020 guidelines for ages 5–17:** "Children and adolescents should limit the amount of time spent being sedentary, particularly the amount of recreational screen time" (strong recommendation, low-certainty evidence). No numeric cap. Higher sedentary time is associated with adiposity, poorer cardiometabolic health, fitness, behavioural conduct and pro-social behaviour, and reduced sleep. The guideline was built on the 2016 Canadian, 2019 Australian and 2018 US guidelines — [Chaput et al. 2020, PMC7691077](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7691077/)
- WHO under-5 guidance (2019) is separate and does set screen maxima for that age group — [WHO](https://who.int/publications/i/item/9789241550536)
- Polish secondary materials commonly cite "under 2: no screens; 2–4: at most 1 h; 5–17: at most 2 h". The 2 h figure for 5–17 is not in WHO's 2020 text above, and some Polish sources give different bands, such as up to 4 h for teens — [search summary of Polish sources, e.g. RMF24](https://www.rmf24.pl/mozg-cialo-twoje-zdrowie-fm/news-do-tego-roku-zycia-dzieci-nie-moga-miec-kontaktu-z-ekranem-e,nId,7675490); [Domowe Zasady Ekranowe PDF](https://wmzdz.pl/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/Domowe_Zasady_Ekranowe.pdf)
- **AAP, February 2026** policy statement and technical report ("Digital Ecosystems, Children, and Adolescents", Pediatrics):
  - "One of the biggest shifts from 2016 to 2026: no set screen time limit."
  - Emphasis on reviewing content quality (Common Sense Media; PBS Kids and Sesame Workshop named as high quality), offering replacement activities, and sharing a family tablet rather than giving a child their own device.
  - Families should build a family media plan (device-free meals as a starter) and address underlying causes of heavy use (boredom, disconnection), screening for ADHD and depression where use is problematic.
  - The policy uses the "5 C's" framework.
  - Co-author Milkovich says the policy aims to "take away some pressure of putting it on parents" and puts more responsibility on the technology.
  - Source: [EdSurge, 5 Feb 2026](https://edsurge.com/news/2026-02-05-new-aap-screen-time-recommendations-focus-less-on-screens-more-on-family-time) (secondary; the Pediatrics text was not read directly)
- **Earlier AAP guidance (2016) for ages 6+:** consistent limits on the type and amount of media; media should not displace sleep, physical activity and other healthy behaviours; media-free times (meals) and places (bedrooms) — [Safety+Health Magazine](https://safetyandhealthmagazine.com/articles/14861-pediatricians-issue-recommendations-on-screen-time-for-kids). EdSurge describes the 2016 guidance as "two hours a day". That figure belongs to older (pre-2016) AAP guidance; the 2016 statement said "consistent limits" for 6+. Conflict noted.
- The AAP Family Media Plan tool covers screen-free zones and times, device curfews, recreational choices, media manners, digital citizenship, safety, sleep and exercise per child — [Children's Mercy summary](https://www.childrensmercy.org/health-care-providers/refer-or-manage-a-patient/connect-with-childrens-mercy/newsletter-the-link/the-link-2025/the-link---july-2025/mental-health-screens-and-sanity-a-pediatricians-guide-to-managing-recreational-media/)
- **Canadian Paediatric Society, "Digital media: Promoting healthy screen use in school-aged children and adolescents"** (Ponti, 6 June 2019; reaffirmed 4 July 2025):
  - Four principles: healthy management, meaningful screen use, positive modelling, and balanced monitoring.
  - Quality and context matter as much as duration; educational, active and social uses are preferred.
  - Warning signs: screen use that interferes with face-to-face interaction, physical activity, school or sleep.
  - Funded by an unrestricted TELUS Wise grant.

### Inferences
- For the parent, the strongest consensus across WHO, AAP 2026 and CPS is about *what* children do and *what it displaces*: sleep, physical activity, homework, family time. A short, active-thinking math game is near the "meaningful, educational" end of the spectrum. It still counts as sedentary screen time and should not run at bedtime or displace outdoor play.
- The Haidt debate centres mainly on social media and phone-based social life for adolescents, not on bounded single-player educational games. Neither side of that debate would classify a no-ads, no-social-feed math game as the main concern. The design lessons both sides would accept: no infinite feed, no notifications pulling children back, no social comparison.
- Polish primary schools will be phone-free during school hours from Sep 2026, so the game will mostly be used at home. That suits a design built around a family-agreed time (the AAP family media plan idea) rather than school play.

## 5. Regulation: DSA Art. 28 and the EU guidelines, GDPR (Poland 16), UK AADC, Apple Kids Category, COPPA, and what a small local-only game must do

### Takeaway
Article 28 DSA, with the July 2025 Commission guidelines, applies to *online platforms* accessible to minors. Micro and small enterprises are exempt (Art. 19), and a standalone single-player game with no user-generated content is very likely not an "online platform" at all. So the DSA probably does not legally bind this project, but its guidelines are the best current EU statement of what "safe by design for minors" means.

GDPR Art. 8 sets the age of digital consent at 16 in Poland. It only bites if the game processes personal data through an information-society service. Keeping progress only on the device and collecting nothing keeps the game largely outside data-protection obligations.

If published on the App Store, Apple requires:
- a privacy policy for every app;
- for the Kids Category, no third-party ads or analytics in principle;
- no links out of the app or purchases except behind a parental gate.

The future EU Digital Fairness Act (draft expected Q3 2026; status unverified) and the November 2025 European Parliament resolution signal that addictive design, streaks and loot boxes for minors are heading toward binding rules.

### Cited Findings
- **DSA Art. 28 guidelines:**
  - Adopted 14 July 2025 (consultation from May 2025). Not legally binding, but the Commission uses them as a benchmark for Art. 28(1) compliance. They apply to online platforms accessible to minors and will be reviewed when necessary, at the latest after 12 months — [Freshfields](https://www.freshfields.com/en/our-thinking/blogs/technology-quotient/dsa-decoded-6-the-european-commission-finalises-guidelines-on-the-protection-of-102kv4s); [EAO Merlin](https://merlin.obs.coe.int/article/10383)
  - Content: disable excessive-use features; push notifications off by default and during sleep hours; private-by-default accounts; limits on behavioural data in recommenders; AI features not on by default; age assurance (self-declaration is not sufficient where age assurance is needed); avoid exploiting commercial illiteracy, including virtual currencies and loot boxes — [Freshfields](https://www.freshfields.com/en/our-thinking/blogs/technology-quotient/dsa-decoded-6-the-european-commission-finalises-guidelines-on-the-protection-of-102kv4s); [Taylor Wessing](https://www.taylorwessing.com/en/insights-and-events/insights/2025/10/beyond-the-tech-giants)
- **DSA Art. 19(1):** "This Section, with the exception of Article 24(3) thereof, shall not apply to providers of online platforms that qualify as micro or small enterprises as defined in Recommendation 2003/361/EC" (micro: fewer than 10 staff and ≤ €2m turnover). VLOPs are covered regardless of size. The minors guidelines apply to online platforms as defined in Art. 3(i) — [Presencis (EUR-Lex text)](https://presencis.com/regulations/dsa/article-19/); [Taylor Wessing](https://www.taylorwessing.com/en/insights-and-events/insights/2025/10/beyond-the-tech-giants); [HLC](https://hlc.com/en/publications/the-longawaited-eu-guidelines-on-article-281-dsa-what-online-platforms-must-know)

- **European Parliament resolution (26 Nov 2025, non-binding):**
  - EU-wide digital minimum age of 16 for social media, video-sharing platforms and AI companions without parental consent, and an absolute minimum of 13.
  - Ban loot boxes and randomised features in games for minors.
  - Default-disable infinite scroll, autoplay, reward loops and harmful gamification.
  - Sources: [Digital Policy Alert](https://digitalpolicyalert.org/event/35723-european-parliament-adopted-resolution-on-protection-of-minors-online-20252060ini); [Kathmandu Post/AFP](https://kathmandupost.com/world/2025/11/26/eu-parliament-pushes-for-age-limits-on-social-media-to-safeguard-minors); [EP text](https://data.europarl.europa.eu/distribution/doc/SP-2026-05-05-TA-10-2025-0299_en.pdf)
- **Digital Fairness Act:**
  - The Commission's fitness check identified dark patterns, addictive design, exploitative personalisation, subscription traps and influencer marketing as problems, with protection of minors a priority.

  - Sources: [Sidley](https://datamatters.sidley.com/2025/08/01/eu-consults-on-digital-fairness-act-big-changes-ahead-for-consumer-facing-platforms/); [Lewis Silkin](https://www.lewissilkin.com/insights/2025/07/18/european-commission-consults-on-digital-fairness-act-102ku4t); [Taylor Wessing](https://www.taylorwessing.com/de/interface/2025/the-dmcca/eu-consumer-law-reform-is-coming-but-when-will-it-happen-and-what-will-it-cover)
- **GDPR Art. 8 in Poland:** for information-society services offered directly to a child, consent-based processing is lawful from age 16. Below 16 a parent must give or authorise consent. ### Inferences
- **DSA applicability:** an "online platform" under DSA Art. 3(i) is a hosting service that disseminates user-provided information to the public. A single-player math game that stores progress locally, with no chat, leaderboards or user-generated content, is very likely not one, and a private individual would in any case be under the micro-enterprise threshold. The 2025 guidelines are therefore best-practice benchmarks here, not legal obligations. If the game later adds public leaderboards, sharing or chat, this needs re-assessment. This is my reading of the definitions, not a cited legal opinion.
- **GDPR and local-only storage:** if no data ever leaves the device, the developer never receives personal data and there is little or nothing to act as "controller" of. Saving game progress locally (localStorage/IndexedDB in a PWA, or on-device storage in an iOS app) at the child's request is the kind of storage the ePrivacy "strictly necessary" exemption is designed for. In practice this means no cookie banner or consent screen is needed for local progress saving. Any analytics, crash reporting sent to a server, remote fonts or CDNs that log IPs, or cloud sync would change this. Even a self-hosted PWA's web server logs IP addresses, which is minimal processing. This is an inference; it should be confirmed against UODO or EDPB text, or with a lawyer, before a public launch.
- **Minimum obligations for a small, free, local-only game** (synthesis):
  - a short, honest privacy statement saying "the game collects no data; progress is stored only on this device". Apple requires a privacy policy link for every app, and the App Store privacy label would be "Data Not Collected";
  - no third-party SDKs (ads, analytics, social login);
  - no push notifications, or at most opt-in and off by default, never at night;
  - no purchases or external links, or put them behind a parental gate;
  - no randomised rewards;
  - no "For Kids" wording on the App Store unless the app enters the Kids Category, which brings Apple's 1.3 rules.
- **COPPA (US)** matters only if the app targets US users under 13 and collects personal information. A game collecting nothing does not trigger its consent requirements; Apple's 5.1.4 cites COPPA as a law to check.
- **Regulatory direction (EP Nov 2025, DFA, EU guidelines):** streaks, reward loops, autoplay, infinite progressions, notifications and random rewards for minors are moving from "frowned upon" toward "default-off or banned". Designing without them now future-proofs the game.

## 6. Concrete do's and don'ts for an ethical but engaging kids' math game (rewards, notifications, session length, parent controls)

### Takeaway
The line is crossed when the game, rather than the child's interest, decides to keep the child playing or bring them back. Rewards are fine if they are predictable, earned, and shown at the end of a task rather than used to block the exit. Notifications are best avoided entirely. Sessions should be short, with natural endings and a gentle break suggestion. Parent controls should be simple, local and behind a basic parental gate. Each item below maps to a cited source from sections 1–5.

### Cited Findings
- Lures at decision points, hidden exits, guilt-tripping characters and fake time pressure are the core manipulative patterns — [Radesky 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9206186/)
- Ads in children's apps, including rewarded ads, are pervasive and confusing to under-8s — [Meyer 2019](https://www.michiganmedicine.org/health-lab/advertising-kids-apps-more-prevalent-parents-may-realize)
- Disable streaks, autoplay and push notifications by default for minors, never send notifications at night, and avoid loot boxes and virtual currencies that exploit commercial illiteracy — [Freshfields on EU guidelines](https://www.freshfields.com/en/our-thinking/blogs/technology-quotient/dsa-decoded-6-the-european-commission-finalises-guidelines-on-the-protection-of-102kv4s); [EAO Merlin](https://merlin.obs.coe.int/article/10383)
- Ban randomised rewards (including prize wheels) and default-disable reward loops and harmful gamification for minors — [EP resolution Nov 2025](https://digitalpolicyalert.org/event/35723-european-parliament-adopted-resolution-on-protection-of-minors-online-20252060ini)
- Natural stopping points help children disengage; autoplay hinders; countdown warnings can backfire for young children — [UW/Hiniker 2016](https://www.washington.edu/news/2016/05/05/two-minute-warnings-make-kids-screen-time-tantrums-worse/)
- Competence, autonomy and relatedness drive enjoyment and continued play — [Ryan, Rigby & Przybylski 2006](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2006/12/061226134706.htm)
- No set time limit; focus on quality, family media plans and shared devices; responsibility belongs with product design — [AAP 2026 via EdSurge](https://edsurge.com/news/2026-02-05-new-aap-screen-time-recommendations-focus-less-on-screens-more-on-family-time)
- Educational, active and social uses are preferred; no screens in the hour before bed — [CPS 2019/2025](https://www.cps.ca/en/documents/position/digital-media)
- Kids-category rules: no third-party ads or analytics, links and purchases only behind a parental gate, and a privacy policy for all apps — [Apple guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- Do not use nudges (big "yes", small "no") to get children to share data or lower protections — [ICO AADC](https://ico.org.uk/media/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services-2-1.pdf)
- Khan Academy Kids shows a free, no-ads, no-subscription educational app can be popular and engaging — [Khan Academy Kids](https://learn.khanacademy.org/khan-academy-kids/)

### Inferences
These are synthesised design rules; each is grounded in the findings above but is my recommendation, not a cited rule.

**DO (engaging without manipulation)**
- **Rewards:**
  - predictable and earned: stars for a completed level, a visible map of progress, badges for mastering a topic;
  - say in advance what finishing a level gives;
  - celebrate effort and improvement ("you solved 3 more than last time"), not only speed.
- **Mastery and challenge:** adaptive difficulty that keeps the child in the zone between bored and frustrated; immediate, kind feedback on mistakes with a hint (competence).
- **Choice:** let the child pick the topic or level and replay any finished level; no forced linear path (autonomy).
- **Family play:** "play together" modes, or a progress screen a parent can look at with the child; offline pride, not online comparison (relatedness).
- **Natural endings:**
  - each level is short, a few minutes, and ends with a calm summary screen;
  - "Finish for today" is at least as visible as "Next level";
  - the next level never auto-starts.
- **Gentle break suggestion:** after a parent-set or default period (e.g. 20–30 minutes; this number is a design choice, not an official guideline), the game suggests a break in a friendly, in-world way and saves progress. It does not punish stopping.
- **Return without pressure:** when the child comes back after days away, greet them warmly ("Welcome back!"), with no lost progress and no "you broke your streak".
- **Privacy by default:** progress stays only on the device; no accounts, no data collection; a one-paragraph privacy note saying so.
- **Parent area behind a simple parental gate** (for example, an arithmetic question for adults or a press-and-hold), where a parent can:
  - set an optional daily soft limit or break reminder;
  - view progress by topic;
  - reset progress;
  - read the privacy note.

**DON'T (where the line is)**
- No ads of any kind, including rewarded ads ("watch a video for a bonus"), and no in-app purchases or virtual currency to buy.
- No random rewards: loot boxes, chests with random contents, spin-the-wheel, gacha-style sticker packs. Even free random rewards borrow gambling mechanics, and the European Parliament wants them banned for minors.
- No consecutive-day streaks that can be "lost", no daily login rewards that grow if you return every day, and no "come back tomorrow or lose X".
- No push notifications or reminders that pull the child back. If ever added, they should be off by default, set by a parent, and never at night.
- No artificial waiting ("next level unlocks in 4 hours"), energy or lives systems that ration play to create return visits, or time-limited events and FOMO countdowns.
- No guilt-tripping characters ("Don't leave me!", "Are you giving up?"), no sad mascot when the child quits, and no shaming for mistakes.
- No hidden or tiny exit buttons, no pop-ups at the moment of quitting, and no infinite or endless mode as the default.
- No public leaderboards or comparison with other children. These also raise DSA and GDPR issues once data leaves the device.
- No third-party SDKs (analytics, ads, social login) and no links out of the app outside the parent area.

**Grey zones (decide consciously)**
- **Collections** (unlocking characters or stickers by completing topics): fine if deterministic and tied to learning milestones; manipulative if randomised or time-limited.
- **Sound and sparkle effects:** fine as feedback for correct answers; questionable if used to draw the child away from the "stop" button at the end of a level (Radesky's "lures").
- **Daily goal** ("today's 5 problems"): fine if optional, with no penalty for skipping and no counter of consecutive days.

### Gaps
- No empirical study was found that tests a specific "ethical engagement" design for school-age math games against an engagement-maximising version, so the do/don't list is a synthesis of regulatory guidance, ages 0–5 content analyses and motivation theory.
- No official recommended session length for educational games for ages 7–15 was found. Any default break timer, such as 20–30 minutes, is a design choice to be agreed with the family, not an official guideline.
